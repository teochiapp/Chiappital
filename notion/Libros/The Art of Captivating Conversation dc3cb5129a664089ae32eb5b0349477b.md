# The Art of Captivating Conversation

Propietario: Teo Chiappero
Etiquetas: Social

I like to boil conversation down to two general purposes:

Entertainment and pleasure

Utility

Conversation Pattern #1: People just want to be ­entertained and receive pleasure from a ­conversation

Conversation Pattern #2: People want validation and to feel like they matter

Conversation Pattern #3: People are extremely lazy

they have the subconscious expectation of being entertained by someone else.

Ask better questions (we’ll go over this), take more initiative in your topics (we’ll go over this too), and overall take a more active and engaged role in the other person

There are two main methods to answer a boring question in an engaging way and create something from nothing.
Method #1: Answer a fuzzy version of the question

Let’s use the example question of “How was your ­weekend?”
The fuzzy version of the question becomes, “Blah blah, weekend?” so all you really understand is the word weekend and that someone is asking about a weekend.

Method #2: Completely re-direct

What you plug in after the pivotal “but . . .” is your opportunity to jump into whatever topic you want that fulfills your goals. You are only limited by your imagination and sense of what the other person will care about.

When you can prepare beforehand, you can hit the ground running and also make sure you present yourself exactly as you want, just like in a job interview.

### The Connecting Attitude

Small talk: it’s like the vegetables of friendly conversation. You may not like it, but you have to get through it to get to the good parts.

You can make more friends in two months by becoming truly interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get other people interested in you

The best way to articulate this is to picture your favorite talk show host.

Making a decision to be genuinely curious and interested in your conversation partner is one of the keys to allowing them to feel comfortable enough to connect to you beyond a superficial level.

The Sherlock Holmes goal is to do the same and make it your goal to learn as much about other people, make observations about them, and put them all together in a few assumptions.

So the first of the three real types of icebreakers occurs when you ask someone for their subjective opinion

Hey, what do you think of the music here? I’m thinking about having a party next weekend and am still building my playlist.

The second method is to ask for objective information.  “Hey, do you know where the host of the party is?”

The final type of icebreaker is to comment on a shared reality. A shared reality is something about the environment that is either observable or known on an unspoken level. “Hey, is it just me or does the music here remind you of a 1970s disco club?”

### Always Knowing What to Say

HPM: History, Philosophy, and Metaphor.

History means you reply with your personal experience regarding a topic.

That reminds me of the last time I skied . . .
That’s just like the first time I skied as a child . . .
What a coincidence, my mother’s friend went skiing last week and had a blast . . . .

Philosophy, on the other hand, involves your personal stance or opinion on a specific topic.

I’ve always loved skiing because . . .
I’ve hated skiing ever since . . .
Skiing is so fun! My favorite hobby.

Metaphor, on the other hand, involves what the conversation topic reminds you of.

That reminds me of . . .
That’s just the opposite of snowboarding, isn’t it?
That makes me think of . . .

Keep in mind that HPM is more focused on you, what you think, and what your experiences are.

SBR: Specific, Broad, and Related

Specific involves asking targeted questions regarding the topic you’re talking about

Broad means you ask broad questions about the topic

Related refers to asking about something that is either directly or broadly related to the subject of your conversation.

EDR Emotion, Detail, and Restatements.

Emotion means when you respond to a statement made in conversation, you state someone else’s emotion or emotional state.
You mention what you believe their emotional response will be. For example, “It seems like you’re really excited about that.

Detail means when you respond to a topic, you do so by asking for details and how they relate to the person with whom you’re speaking. who, what, where, when, and why

Restatements means when you respond to a topic, you do so by restating or summarizing what the other person said and then throw it back at them. When you restate what they say, you are essentially validating them twice. First, the simple act of summarizing what they said already validates them.

### Break the Banter

Art of Misconstruing: misunderstanding people in an intentional manner to bring about a comical situation. Playing dumb or confused and taking an entirely different meaning from what someone has said on purpose.

blowing up somebody’s statement to an absurd and exaggerated form.
Another example: “That coffee was terrible!” You could reply, “I agree, my car’s battery water is tastier.”

“I love my television,” you might reply, “So do your parents know that you guys are living together before marriage?”

If you want to use this technique, make sure you really blow it up and make it out of this world. That way it’s obvious to the other person that you are making fun and they can laugh along.

Sarcasm functions like a social cue: a way to express something without having to explicitly say it.

Bob: “It’s so hot today!”
You: “I see you’re a meteorologist in training.”

Bob: “I got an F on my math test.”
You: “Now I know who to call when my calculator breaks.”

### Conversation Threading

Step one: break the statement down into specific elements.

Step two: comment on or ask a question about any of those elements.

if you can finish each other’s sentences, you can create the feeling of familiarity and closeness.

The Four-Word Magic Phrase: “What were you saying?”

Try, as much as possible, not to interrupt others. But when you do, saying “What were you saying?” can be a life-saver for the conversation.

Leading the interaction means what it sounds like—it’s when you take responsibility for the conversation and take on the burden of making sure it flows.
