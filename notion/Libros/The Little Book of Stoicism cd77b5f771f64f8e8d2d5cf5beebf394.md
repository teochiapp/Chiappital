## Chapter 1: The Promise of Stoic Philosophy

Stoicism has nothing to do with suppressing or hiding one’s emotions or being emotionless. Rather, it’s about acknowledging our emotions, reflecting on what causes them, and learning to redirect them for our own good.

You need to go out in the real world and train like a warrior-philosopher.

### The Stoic Happiness Triangle in a Nutshell

![Untitled](The%20Little%20Book%20of%20Stoicism/Untitled.png)

Eudaimonia: At the core of the triangle is eudaimonia—the ultimate goal of life all ancient philosophies agreed on. It’s about living a supremely happy and smoothly flowing life. It’s about thriving in our lives.

### 1. Live with Areté: Express your highest self in every moment.

Separate good from bad and focus on what we control.

Focus on What You Control: This is the most prominent principle in Stoicism.

Take Responsibility: Good and bad come solely from yourself. It’s not events that make us happy or miserable, but our interpretation of those events

In whatever you do, imagine there are two lines: the higher line indicating what you’re capable of and the lower line what you’re actually doing.

### The Four Cardinal Virtues

Wisdom is about understanding how to act and feel appropriately.

Justice is about knowing how to act and feel well in our relationships with others.

Courage is about knowing how to act and feel correctly when facing fearful situations.

Self-Discipline (or temperance) is about knowing how to act and feel right, despite emotions such as strong desire, inner resistance, or lust.

Attention (literally): If we want to be the best we can be in every situation, if we want to live with areté, then we need to be aware of our every step. Today, we call this “mindfulness,” the Stoics used the term “attention” (prosochê).

> Attention (prosochê) is the fundamental Stoic spiritual attitude,
> 

> It is a continuous vigilance and presence of mind, self-consciousness which never sleeps, and a constant tension of the spirit.
> 

### 2. Focus on What You Control: Accept Whatever Happens and Make the Best of It

Make the best use of what is in our power, and take the rest as it happens.

> That’s the root cause of emotional suffering, to worry about outside events.
> 

### Stoic dichotomy of control

High influence: Our choices in judgments and actions
Partial influence: Health, wealth, relationships, and outcomes of our behaviors
No influence: Weather, ethnicity, and most external circumstances

Focus on the process (under our control), instead of the desired outcome (not under our control).

The Stoic archer succeeds in the process and is ready to take any outcome with equanimity and calm confidence, knowing they’ve tried their very best.

A warrior takes everything as a challenge to become their best, while an ordinary person just takes everything either as a blessing or curse.

So the cards don’t matter, they’re neutral, indifferent. What matters is how well you play them.

### 3. Take Responsibility: Get Good from Yourself

The final corner of the Stoic Happiness Triangle is built upon the two others; they make this third corner possible.

This results in us being responsible for our own happy lives.

The Stoics argue that you can step in between what happens (dog shit), and your reaction to it (anger and f-words). The idea is to choose your best virtuous response rather than going with the default.

You cannot be harmed unless you let it, because the other person has no access to your mind.

Don’t be satisfied with mere learning, but practice, practice, practice! Because if time passes, Epictetus says, we forget what we’ve learned and end up doing the opposite, and hold opinions the opposite of what we should.

### Practices

When you plan to do something, you add the caveat “if nothing prevents me.”

“I’ll do my best but the outcome is ultimately not within my control. I can’t be absolutely certain that it will come out as planned, but I’ll try my best.”

In Stoicism, that’s always a chance to practice some virtue: courage, humility, reason, justice, patience, self-discipline, and forgiveness.

“Think of yourself as dead,” says Marcus Aurelius, “you have lived your life. Now take what’s left and live properly.

It’s about periodically reflecting on the fact that you will not live forever, you’re mortal, and you might not wake up the next morning.

You won’t take things for granted anymore, and appreciate every little thing much more. You will savor each and every moment. Because you’re well aware that all these things had not been granted to you indefinitely. Think of all you’ve got as borrowed: your best friend, spouse, kids, cat, health, status, car, and laptop.

In the end, we come with nothing, and go with nothing.

Negative visualization is an imagination exercise in which you foresee bad stuff. It prepares you to stay calm and deal effectively with whatever life will throw at you.

> What could go wrong?
What obstacle could pop up?
Where could I face difficulties?
> 

Voluntary Discomfort: Basically, you practice getting comfortable with what you would now describe as uncomfortable.

Be creative with this idea: Drink only water for a day. Eat for less than $3 a day for a week. Try fasting for a day or two. Wear old and dirty clothes. Spend a month on a tight budget. If you’re hardcore, spend a night under a bridge.

Train now when it’s still easy, and you’ll be prepared for when it gets tough.

### Prepare Yourself for the Day: The Stoic Morning Routine

You do not prepare to be against the world, you prepare to act reasonably within a chaotic world where not everybody is as well prepared as you are

Modify the Stoics’ morning routines to your liking; maybe you want to form a plan for the day or maybe you want to give yourself a pep talk, maybe you want to exercise, meditate, or journal, and maybe you want to sing under the shower. Feel free, just make sure to keep a regular morning routine.

### Review Your Day: The Stoic Evening Routine

> I examine my entire day, going through what I have done and said. I conceal nothing from myself, I pass nothing by. I have nothing to fear from my errors when I can say: ‘See that you do not do this anymore. For the moment, I excuse you.’
> 

Sit down with your journal and review: What did you do? What did you well? What not so well? How could you improve?

> Good: What did I do well today?
Better: How could I improve? What could I do better?
Best: What do I need to do if I want to be the best version of myself?
> 

### Keep a Role Model in Mind:

It can be an idol like Roger Federer, a superhero like Batman, or just a person you admire like your mom or dad. Imagine this person to constantly be watching you and your actions. This will bring more awareness into your daily life and enable you to choose your actions more deliberately.

### Eliminate the Nonessential

> “Most of what we say and do is not essential. If you can eliminate it, you’ll have more time, and more tranquility. Ask yourself at every moment, ‘Is this necessary?’ But we need to eliminate unnecessary assumptions as well. To eliminate the unnecessary actions that follow.”
> 

If you seek tranquility, do less . . . do what’s essential.” This will bring a double satisfaction, says Marcus Aurelius, “to do less, better.”

Let’s get better at what matters. Let’s learn how to deal with depressive thoughts, how to be a good listener, how to stay calm in the face of adversity, and how to be a good spouse, parent, and friend.

### Forget Fame:

By seeking social status, we give other people power over us. We have to act in a calculated way to make them admire us, and we must refrain from doing things in their disfavor. We enslave ourselves by seeking fame.

> News is all about worrying about stuff outside our control
> 

### Become an Eternal Student

1. Be humble: As Epictetus teaches us, “It is impossible to learn that which one thinks one already knows.” And Marcus adds, “If anyone can prove and show to me that I think and act in error, I will gladly change it—for I seek the truth.”

2. Put it into practice: Don’t be satisfied with mere learning, Epictetus warns us, “For as time passes we forget and end up doing the opposite.” As warriors of the mind, we must go out and actually live out what we’ve learned.

> “It’s possible, for a person who has had a long life to have lived too little.”
> 

> “Everybody has a plan until they get punched in the face.”
> 
- People who mean everything to you will die.
- You will lose your job.
- You will get sick.
- You will face critical life decisions.
- Your favorite mug will break.
- You will feel miserably depressed for no obvious reason.
- The weather and the world will seem to be against you.

Life gets tough. The following practices and strategies will help you deal with it effectively:

### Situational Practices: How to Deal with Yourself when Life Gets Tough?

You are disturbed not by what happens, but by your opinion about it

Don’t judge the event as good or bad, just take it as it is—and you won’t get harmed.

Face the emotion, and get out of the hole. At some point the negative feeling will feed from itself, like a vicious cycle.

We must stop attaching ourselves to external things and desires which are not under our control. Because a lack of control leads to fear. He who does not desire anything outside his control cannot be anxious.

As aspiring Stoics, we should try to see the world as it really is, rather than demanding that it fits our expectations.

While other people see adversity as bad, as something preventing them from achieving their goals, we recognize the opportunity for growth and flip it around—we see opportunity where they see evil.

We should catch ourselves whenever we get thrown off-balance by some event, and then get back to a balanced mind as quickly as possible.

The question is not if life will throw some punches at you, but when. And how you’ll respond to it.

Next time something inconveniently happens to you, imagine it happened to someone else. Ask yourself how you’d react when the same happened to your colleague Sharon. If it’s not terrible when it happens to Sharon, then it’s not terrible when it happens to you.

> “Moving on to graver things: when somebody’s wife or child dies, to a man we all routinely say, ‘Well, that’s part of life.’ But if one of our own family is involved, then right away it’s ‘Poor, poor me!’”
> 

Your massive problem suddenly gets utterly insignificant compared to the vastness of the universe.

### Situational Practices: How to Handle Yourself When Other People Challenge You?

And how should we respond when confronted?
The Stoics say with humor rather than a counter insult. Make a joke, laugh it off.

“Who then is invincible? The one who cannot be upset by anything outside their reasoned choice.”

Don’t gossip. Don’t blame. Don’t complain. Don’t talk too much. Especially not about what’s not meaningful.

> “Acquire the habit of attending carefully to what is being said by another, and of entering, so far as possible, into the mind of the speaker.”
> 

Resist the urge to speak. Accept that something within you always wants to respond immediately.

> "Waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one.”
>
